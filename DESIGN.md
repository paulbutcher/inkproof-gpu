# Inkproof.Gpu design

## Command set and wire format

`Inkproof.Gpu.specs` is the one definition of the command set: each command has a name, an opcode and a list of operand kinds, and nothing in it names a graphics API. The Lean encoder and decoder are driven by the table, and `Inkproof.Gpu.js` prints the JavaScript decoder and interpreter from it, so they cannot drift. The WebGL2 realisation of each command lives only in the JavaScript printer (`webglBody`), which is where a WebGPU backend would differ.

A command list is one buffer: a little-endian 32-bit command count, then each command as an opcode byte followed by its operands. `generated/gpu.js` is the printed output; `lake exe gen generated` regenerates it. The GLSL shaders beside it and the JavaScript tests fail when it is stale. `createProgram` builds the renderer's one program from the shader sources embedded in `gpu.js`; `uniform` names a uniform by its position in `uniformNames` and sets it with as many floats as it is given, and `draw` takes the buffer, the vertex count and the vertex stride in words: two floats for each attribute of the vertex shader in order, `a_position`, `a_dir` and `a_corner`, with a stride of 2 giving the last two their constant defaults (`attributeDefaults`) and a stride of 6 reading all three.

## Compiling a display list

`compile` turns a view's tiles (a display list, the transform uniforms and a map from clip rectangles to scissor words) into `Op`s; `Op.toCmd` gives the wire command. The caller supplies `tess`, the vertex words of one command, and its raster fidelity is outside the theorems. Buffers are cached by content, so a tile whose vertex words match a cached entry reuses its buffers and pan and zoom upload nothing. Unused buffers are deleted and handles are never reused. A frame sets the state (the three uniforms and the scissor) and draws once per command, in layer order; the host binds the program. The scissor is four Int32 words (x, y, width, height in device pixels from the bottom left) that `Tile.scissor` computes from a clip rectangle in grid coordinates, or none for no restriction; a command whose clips leave nothing gets an empty scissor. `clipRect_region` states that no point of a shape lies outside the rectangle it is scissored to.

The theorems are stated against `Gpu` in `Proofs/Compile.lean`, an abstract model of buffers, uniform and draw log. Handles go on the wire modulo `2 ^ 32`, so the model is exact only while fewer than that many buffers are allocated.

## Frame of a pixel-grid chart

`pixelTile w h list` is the tile of a display list in device pixels from the top left of a `w` by `h` target, with origin at the top left pixel: slope `(2/w, -2/h)` and offset `(-1, 1)` rounded to Float32, taken from the `Scale.Affine` maps `pixelX` and `pixelY` (`pixelX_apply` and `pixelY_apply` state the exact maps, `2x/w - 1` and `1 - 2y/h`), and a scissor that flips y and clamps to the target (`pixelBox_covers`). `frame` is `frameSetup` (build the program when the context is fresh, use it, lift the scissor, clear) followed by `compile` of that one tile; the scissor is lifted first because `clear` respects it. `frame_roundtrip` states that it survives encode and decode.

`viewTile w h view plot list` is the tile of a display list in data coordinates. `View` holds the visible x and y ranges (y upwards); `viewX` and `viewY` are the `Scale.Linear` from each range to the matching side of `plot`, as `Scale.Affine`, which is the data to screen map of inkproof-viewport's intervals (`View` here carries only the four bounds, a render input); they map onto `plot`, a rectangle in pixels from the top left, and the tile's uniforms are `pixelX` and `pixelY` composed with them and rounded once to Float32 (`viewClip_x`, `viewClip_y`). Every shape is clipped to the view (`View.clip`), which gives the plot its scissor through `viewScissor`; a tessellator that ignores clips yields the same vertex words under every view (`viewTile_cmds`), so `viewFrame` under another view uploads nothing (`viewFrame_cached`). A theorem does not bound the Float32 rounding of the uniforms; a test checks half a pixel.

`viewTile` has stride 6 and expects `tessellateStroke`'s vertex words (the tile of `pixelTile` has stride 2 and `tessellate`'s). In `tessellateStroke` a strip's half width and a marker's radius are not in the words: the words give each vertex its centre-line point, the direction of its segment and a corner, and the size, in pixels, is the `u_size` uniform that `Tile.size` supplies per command (`strokeSize`). The vertex shader maps the direction to the screen, normalises it there and moves the vertex by the size times its corner along and across it, so the line is as wide in every direction under any view, and the buffers do not depend on the view (`viewFrame_stroke_cached`). The strip's rectangle is exact where Scene's `stripTriangles` uses a length bound within a factor of `√2`, so it is at most that much wider than the shader's; the shader's agrees with the exact rectangle to half a pixel (a test). A zero-width view gives a zero direction on screen and a vertex that is not drawn.

`viewOverlayFrame` compiles a `viewTile` and a `pixelStrokeTile` (a `pixelTile` with stroke vertices and sizes) in one `compile`, since `compile` deletes the buffers no tile of its call uses and two calls would delete each other's. Under another view the data's buffers are kept (`viewOverlayFrame_data_cached`), and with the same overlay nothing is uploaded (`viewOverlayFrame_cached`); a changed overlay uploads only its own buffers.

## Tessellation

`tessellate origin` is the `tess` that `compile` takes: the vertex words of one command, as three vertices per triangle, each vertex two Float32 words (x then y, little-endian on the wire like all words) holding the position relative to `origin`, a point in grid coordinates that the caller also uses as the tile origin. Coordinates are converted from Scene's exact rationals to Float32 after subtracting the origin, so a tile's words do not depend on where it sits.

`triangles` gives the triangles in grid coordinates. Rectangles, text boxes and square markers are two triangles; a strip is Scene's `stripTriangles`; an area or envelope is two triangles per column pair; a circle marker is an octagon fan that contains the circle. A clipped shape gives the triangles of the shape it clips; the scissor keeps the drawing inside the clip box.

`region_sub_triangles` states that every point of a polygonal shape lies in one of its triangles, and `covers_triangles` that a command's covered pixels each meet a triangle. Circles are excluded: the octagon containing the circle is checked by a test, not proved. How a GPU rasterises a triangle, which pixels it samples, is outside the theorems.

The renderer's uniforms, numbered in this order, are `u_slope` and `u_offset` (two floats each), `u_size` (one float, a stroke's size in pixels), `u_view` (four floats: half the target's width and height, then their reciprocals doubled, from `viewWords`), `u_colour` (four floats, the command's colour as red, green, blue and alpha in 0 to 1, which for a gradient is the colour of its first stop) and the gradient uniforms of the next section. For a map from grid coordinates to clip space `clip = A * p + B` computed in double precision, a tile with origin `o` takes `u_slope = A` and `u_offset = A * o + B`, so that `(p - o) * u_slope + u_offset = clip`. Before each draw `compile` sets `u_slope` and `u_offset` from the tile (`Tile.slope`, `Tile.offset`), `u_size` and `u_view` likewise, `u_colour` from the command's colour (`colourWords`), the gradient uniforms, and the scissor.

## Gradients

A `Scene.Shading.gradient` varies a colour along x or y of the grid, piecewise linearly between stops, and `shadingState` turns a shading into `u_colour` and a `Gradient`: `u_axis` (the unit vector of the axis), and for each of `gradientSegments` (4) segments between adjacent stops its start along the axis relative to the tile origin (`u_start`, in the tile's own coordinates like the vertices), the reciprocal of its length (`u_inv`) and the change of colour across it (`u_delta0` to `u_delta3`). The vertex shader passes the vertex position on as `v_pos`; the fragment shader takes `t` as `v_pos` along the axis and gives `u_colour` plus each segment's change times `clamp((t - start) * inv, 0, 1)`. A flat shading has zero changes, so it draws as it always did. A segment of no length is a step (`stepInv`), and a segment a gradient does not use has zeros. A gradient with more than `gradientSegments + 1` stops, or whose stops descend, is not drawn: its command gets an empty scissor (`Tile.stateOf_unsupported`), not a wrong colour.

The fragment position is the interpolated vertex position, so a fill is shaded at each pixel's centre, as `Shading.at` is. A strip or marker takes the position of its centre line, not of the pixel the shader offsets it to, so across a stroke's width its colour is constant where Scene's would vary by the gradient's slope times the half width. `evalStops_close` states, in exact arithmetic, that the shader's blend of each channel is within half a unit of Scene's rounded one; a test draws gradient and flat fills in headless Chromium and checks each pixel against `Shading.at` to 1 in 255, which also covers the Float32 rounding that no theorem bounds.

A draw sets the gradient uniforms only where they differ from the previous draw's (`Gradient.ops`, `itemsOps`), and all of them for the first draw of a call, since what the GL context holds from an earlier frame is unknown, so a frame of flat commands pays for them once.

## Shader language

`Inkproof.Gpu.Shader` is a small Float32 language: scalars and vectors, componentwise `+`, `-` and `*` with scalar broadcast, vector construction and component selection. A `Shader` is typed inputs, uniforms and outputs, local declarations and assignments. `Shader.run` gives its meaning over inkproof-float's Float32 and `Shader.glsl` prints GLSL ES 3.00; the renderer's shaders (`vertexShader`, `fragmentShader`) are values of this type and `generated/*.glsl` are printed from them. Division and fused multiply-add are not in the language.

The vertex shader maps a position relative to its tile origin through the rebased affine map `a_position * u_slope + u_offset`, with the slope and offset computed in double precision by the caller, and adds the stroke offset of the vertex's corner in pixels (`u_view` converts between clip space and pixels). The language has `rsqrt`, the reciprocal square root of a scalar, for the direction's length, and `sat`, a scalar clamped to 0 to 1, for the gradient's fractions.

### What is not proved

The printer preserves meaning only under the Float32 model of inkproof-float, which assumes IEEE binary32 rounding for `+`, `-` and `*`. GLSL ES 3.00 guarantees far less for `highp` (a relative precision of 2^-16 and no stated rounding), so no theorem can relate the printed text to the language under the specification's own precision model; the placement bound holds on hardware that implements binary32.

The half-pixel placement bound (`vertexShader_placement`) is proved directly from inkproof-float's Float32 rounding lemmas, for the two operations that place a vertex's centre; the stroke offset added to it is not bounded by a theorem, and a test checks it against the exact corners. The origin is subtracted on the CPU in double precision, so the rebased affine theorem of inkproof-scale, which models the subtraction in the same precision, does not describe this shader; the bound is on the Float32 inputs the shader receives.
